# Vection experiment — first-person dot grid

This script implements a **visual vection** paradigm: a first-person display in darkness where optic flow from a floor dot grid is driven by the keyboard to induce the **conscious subjective experience of self-motion** (vection) without landmarks.

---

## What is vection?

Following Palmisano et al. (2015), *vection* is most often defined as:

> **A visual illusion of self-motion in a stationary observer** — induced by moving, or simulating the motion of, the surrounding environment.

The common thread across definitions is the **conscious subjective experience of self-motion**: the feeling that *you* are moving, even when you are not (or when that feeling is driven mainly by vision).

- **Linear vection (LV):** illusion of self-*translation* (e.g. moving forward/backward).
- **Circular vection (CV):** illusion of self-*rotation* (e.g. turning left/right).

Vision is especially effective at inducing vection: *“highly compelling illusions of self-motion can be generated by presenting large patterns of optic flow to physically stationary observers”* (Palmisano et al., 2015, citing Dichgans and Brandt, 1978; Riecke, 2010). Classic examples include a rotating drum with stripes (circular vection) or simulated forward motion (linear vection). The script uses **optic flow only** — no landmarks — so self-motion is conveyed purely by the pattern of motion on the retina.

**Reference:**  
Palmisano, S., Allison, R. S., Schira, M. M., & Barry, R. J. (2015). Future challenges for vection research: definitions, functional significance, measures, and neural bases. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 193. doi: 10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00193

## How this script generates vection

The script is designed to elicit **linear and circular vection** in a minimal, first-person setup:

1. **Darkness and no landmarks**  
   The display is otherwise dark. There are no static objects or landmarks — only a grid of dots on the floor. Self-motion is signaled purely by **optic flow** (the pattern of dot motion on the screen), consistent with the idea that large-field optic flow is a key driver of vection (Palmisano et al., 2015).

2. **First-person optic flow**  
   The observer sees the scene from a first-person perspective. When you press **forward (7)**, the dot pattern moves backward on the screen (as if the world were flowing past you). When you **rotate (6/9)**, the dots rotate in the opposite direction. This matches the retinal flow that would occur during real translation and rotation, so the brain can interpret it as self-motion (linear or circular vection).

3. **Dots only while moving, with limited depth**  
   Dots are shown only while a movement key is held, and only **nearby** dots within a limited depth range are drawn. There is no distant “horizon” of dots. This keeps the stimulus local and avoids a stable reference frame, which can strengthen the sense that *you* are moving rather than the environment.

4. **Fade-in and fade-out**  
   Dots fade in when movement starts and fade out when it stops. The transition is smooth rather than abrupt, which can make the onset of optic flow (and thus vection) feel more natural.

5. **Linear and circular vection**  
   - **Keys 7 and 8** produce forward/backward translation → **linear vection**.  
   - **Keys 6 and 9** produce left/right rotation → **circular vection**.

In short: the script simulates first-person movement through a dark environment by drawing a patch of floor dots whose motion is consistent with that movement. That motion is pure optic flow with no landmarks, which is the type of visual stimulation that vection research (e.g. Palmisano et al., 2015) links to the conscious experience of self-motion.

## Controls

| Key | Action        |
|-----|---------------|
| 6   | Rotate left   |
| 7   | Move forward  |
| 8   | Move backward |
| 9   | Rotate right  |
| Esc | Quit          |

Both the **number row** (6–9) and the **numpad** are supported.

```bash
cd exploration_trigger_vection
pip install pygame
python run_vection_pygame.py
```

## Config

Edit constants at the top of `run_vection_pygame.py` (or `run_vection.py`) to change:

- `MOVE_SPEED`, `TURN_SPEED` — movement and rotation speed
- `Z_FAR`, `DOTS_VISIBLE_RADIUS` — depth and extent of visible dots (affects strength of flow)
- `DOT_ALPHA`, `FADE_IN_SPEED`, `FADE_OUT_SPEED` — dot visibility and fade
- `EYE_HEIGHT`, `FOV_DEG` — viewpoint and field of view
- `DOT_RADIUS_PX` — dot size in pixels (Pygame version)

---

## Fullscreen (Pygame)

In `run_vection_pygame.py`, change `set_mode` to:

`screen = pygame.display.set_mode((0, 0), pygame.FULLSCREEN)`

Larger, more immersive displays can increase vection strength (e.g. Palmisano et al., 2015; Dichgans & Brandt, 1978).
